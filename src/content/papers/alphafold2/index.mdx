## 0. Prelims

Throughout this guide, we'll use **minAlphaFold2**, a compact reimplementation of AlphaFold2 in PyTorch. The repository is available at https://github.com/ChrisHayduk/minAlphaFold2.

The DeepMind implementation is a full research codebase with the supporting features required for training and inference. minAlphaFold2 has a narrower purpose: it keeps the central operations in a smaller codebase and makes their correspondence with the paper explicit.

**Variable names follow the paper.** `m` is the MSA representation, with shape $(s, r, c_m)$, where $s$ is the number of sequences, $r$ is the number of residues, and $c_m$ is the channel dimension. `z` is the pair representation, with shape $(r, r, c_z)$. `s_i` is the single representation in the Structure Module. `T_i` is a rigid frame. This makes it easier to compare the implementation with the paper and supplement.

**Algorithms are annotated inline.** Every major class and function in minAlphaFold2 cites the supplement algorithm it implements. Comments like `# Algorithm 7` appear right above the relevant forward method, allowing you to reference the description and pseudocode from the paper while reading the code. When I say "Algorithm 7" below, you can grep for it in the repo to find the corresponding code. Shapes are typically asserted throughout the forward pass, and the tests exercise those assertions end-to-end.

**There are three config profiles** living under `configs/`, intended to provide runnable versions of minAlphaFold2 for a range of compute budgets:

- `tiny.toml` — about 90k parameters, CPU-runnable, good for unit tests and for the overfit walkthrough in §14.
- `medium.toml` — for overfit experiments on a single GPU.
- `alphafold2.toml` — the full 48-block, paper-spec config.

The walkthrough below will mostly stay at the level of the architecture, but when we get to the concrete example in §14, we'll load `tiny.toml` and watch the Structure Module actually converge on a real protein.

## 1. Background

AlphaFold2 was not the first deep-learning approach to protein structure prediction, but its CASP14 results represented a large improvement over prior computational methods. For many targets, its predictions approached the accuracy of experimentally determined structures. Understanding the architecture first requires some biological context, particularly the relationship between sequence, evolution, and structure.

### 1.1 What are proteins

Let's start with a seemingly simple question - what are proteins and why should we care about them?

Proteins are large molecules produced within the cells of living organisms to perform some function. You can think of proteins as the machinery of life. They are responsible for facilitating chemical reactions in the body, providing structural integrity to cells, acting as receptors, and more. Quite simply, life as we know it would not be possible without the vast array of protein-based nanomachines constantly at work in our bodies.

The functions that a protein takes on (e.g., whether it acts as a receptor attached to a cell membrane or as a catalyst for a chemical reaction) come down in large part to its 3D structure. The shape of the protein determines what other molecules it can interact with, thereby determining what it can *do* in the body.

<LactaseFigure />

Now, if proteins are so important, it may be natural to ask "where do they come from?" And herein lies the central role of DNA in living organisms.

It is a common cliché to hear that DNA is the system of record for an organism, or the set of instructions for life. But it is less often said exactly *what* instructions DNA is providing. 

DNA encodes instructions for making proteins. It also helps control which proteins a cell produces and when. First, a gene is transcribed into RNA. Ribosomes then read groups of three RNA bases, called codons, to build an amino-acid chain. Most codons specify an amino acid; stop codons signal the end of translation.

<CentralDogmaDiagram />

<Sidenote>
Biologists often refer to the amino acid sequence of a protein as its primary structure and to its 3D structure as its tertiary structure.
</Sidenote>

Hence, from two different views, a protein is simultaneously a one-dimensional string of amino acids and also a three-dimensional, interactive structure. How can we reconcile these two views?

They're connected by what's known as **protein folding**.

### 1.2 The protein folding problem

Protein folding is the process by which the one-dimensional amino acid sequence output by the ribosomes folds into its ultimate 3D shape. As we discussed earlier, this shape determines the functions that the protein can fulfill in the body. Thus, function follows structure, and structure follows sequence.

In practice, determining a protein's amino acid sequence is much easier, faster, and cheaper than determining its 3D structure, so it would be nice to be able to predict the structure from the sequence. Unfortunately, this task was historically nearly impossible.

<Sidenote>
  Anfinsen showed this experimentally in 1961 by denaturing ribonuclease A and
  letting it refold. It folded back to the same shape every time. The Nobel
  Prize followed in 1972.
</Sidenote>

The **protein folding problem** is hard for the reason Cyrus Levinthal pointed out in 1969: a sequence of length 100 has something like $3^{100}$ possible conformations if each residue has three rotational states. The search space is too large for brute-force enumeration, yet proteins fold reliably on biological timescales.

The only way this can happen is if the energy landscape is highly biased. Rather than sample uniformly from all possible conformations, proteins are being funneled by the physics of their interactions toward a small set of low-energy structures.

This insight opens the door to modeling the funneling dynamics present in protein folding. For decades, the field tried to model that funnel directly through molecular dynamics, statistical potentials, fragment libraries, and many other approximations. These methods worked in pieces, but not well enough at the proteome scale due to their immense computational cost. Running a full molecular dynamics simulation for large proteins was just computationally intractable.

AlphaFold2's core innovation was to stop trying to explicitly simulate the entire physical process and instead learn the shape of the funnel from data. We'll discuss two of these key data sources in the next two subsections (1.2 and 1.3).

### 1.3 MSAs and evolutionary coupling

The key insight that unlocks protein folding, which predates AlphaFold2 by decades, is that related proteins across species tend to share the same domains, and the mutations they accumulate over evolutionary time are not independent. If amino acids $i$ and $j$ in the protein sequence are in contact in the 3D structure, then a mutation at $i$ that disrupts that contact creates selection pressure for a compensating mutation at $j$. That is, if $j$ doesn't mutate to compensate for the mutation at $i$, then the protein won't successfully fold into the shape needed to achieve its function. Over millions of years and many species, that pressure leaves a statistical signature - the columns for residues $i$ and $j$ in a multiple sequence alignment co-vary.

A multiple sequence alignment, or MSA, is exactly what it sounds like. We take the target sequence, find a large set of evolutionarily related sequences, and align them so that homologous residues are in the same column.

(Terminology note: homologous == "evolutionarily related positions in the amino acid sequences", residue == "a single amino acid in a sequence")

We can see an example of a small MSA below. The rows represent related proteins from various species. Each column is an aligned position in those sequences (e.g., amino acid 1 is in the first column).

From an MSA, you can infer a surprising amount. Column variation tells you which residues are conserved, meaning evolution has decided they matter, and which residues are free to drift. 

(Another terminology note: in biology speak, "conserved residues" just means amino acids in the same position that don't change across species in the MSA.)

Correlated columns tell you which residues are coupled, which is often the signature of contact in 3D. This is why AlphaFold2 treats the MSA as a first-class input rather than as a side feature - these coupled residues give the model a strong starting point to "guess" which residues should be next to each other in the 3D structure.

AlphaFold2 builds its MSAs by searching four databases with two tools (HHBlits for sensitivity, JackHMMER for coverage):

- **MGnify** — metagenomic sequences, where a lot of novel protein diversity lives.

- **BFD** — Big Fantastic Database, billions of sequences assembled from raw metagenomic reads.

### 1.4 Templates

With MSAs, we are looking at sequences that are evolutionarily similar to our target sequence in order to find correlations in individual amino acids. But if we already know the solved 3D structure of one of these similar proteins? If its sequence is very close to our target, shouldn't its structure be close as well?

This is where templates come in, forming the second source of prior knowledge AlphaFold2 uses. If an evolutionarily related protein already has a solved 3D structure in the Protein Data Bank, its coordinates can be used as a hint for the model to "guess" at the coordinates of the target protein. AlphaFold2 finds up to four templates via HHSearch on PDB70, converts them into pair-like embeddings, and injects them into the same broad pipeline as the MSA (i.e., the Evoformer).

Hence, while we can think of MSA as giving us granular information on which pairs. of residues are in contact, we can think of templates as giving us global information about what the entire folded protein might look like.

In practice within AlphaFold2, templates are mostey useful when evolutionary information is sparse (that is, when the MSA search for a protein does not return many hits), but the model does not depend on them in the way older template-based methods did. The MSA and the learned pair representation do most of the work. As such, we will treat templates as an important input-pipeline detail and cover them briefly in §8, rather than making them the center of the story.

### 1.5 Rigid frames and SE(3)

AlphaFold2 outputs a 3D structure. Its most important intermediate object is a **local frame** associated with each residue: a position $t_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and a rotation $R_i \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ that together specify a small coordinate system attached to the backbone. These frames, one per residue, represent the whole structure.

This matters for two reasons. First, frames let us express geometric invariances. The physics of protein folding does not care about the global position or orientation of the molecule. If you translate and rotate the entire structure, it is still the same protein. That is SE(3) invariance, and any loss or model component that respects it has a much easier problem than one that has to learn it from scratch. Second, frames also let us express *local* geometry. The position of residue $j$ *as seen by residue $i$* is just $T_i^{-1} \cdot t_j$, meaning the translation $t_j$ in residue $i$'s local coordinate system. That "as seen by" operation is the core of Invariant Point Attention, which we'll get to in §10.

We mostly won't need frames in §§1–9; we'll return to them in the Structure Module. The Evoformer updates the pair representation $z$ to give the Structure Module the information it needs to place atoms in space.

### 1.6 What AlphaFold2 changed

The rough outline of a contact-prediction-plus-folding pipeline had existed for years: run an MSA, extract co-evolution signals, predict a residue-residue contact map, then fold the protein through fragment assembly or distance-based minimization. AlphaFold1 was already doing something in this family. AlphaFold2 introduced the following changes:

1. **End-to-end training.** No separate "predict contacts, then fold" pipeline. The entire system, from MSA input to atom coordinates, is one computation graph with one loss.
2. **The Evoformer.** A transformer-style stack that cross-attends between the MSA representation and the pair representation, letting each update the other over 48 blocks.
3. **Invariant Point Attention.** A geometric attention variant that operates on rigid frames, respects SE(3) invariance by construction, and lets the Structure Module reason about 3D without losing equivariance.
4. **FAPE loss.** A frame-aligned loss that measures error per-residue-frame rather than in a global coordinate system, giving every residue a local view of the structure it's trying to build.
5. **Recycling.** A mechanism that feeds the model's own output predictions back as input for another forward pass, enabling iterative refinement at both train and test time.

The overview below follows the path from sequence data to atom coordinates. Read it from top to bottom: inputs become MSA and pair representations, the Evoformer updates both, and the Structure Module builds a structure. ## 2. Input representations

AlphaFold2 takes in three kinds of raw data (the target sequence, the MSA, and the templates) and distills them into two working representations that flow through the rest of the network:

- the **MSA representation** $m$, shape $(s, r, c_m)$, where $s$ is the number of sequences in the alignment and $r$ is the number of residues.
- the **pair representation** $z$, shape $(r, r, c_z)$, with one embedding for every pair of residues in the target sequence.

You can think of $m$ as "what we know about each residue, as seen through evolutionary history" and $z$ as "what we know about each pair of residues." The Evoformer passes information back and forth between these two objects: it uses the pair representation to guide attention within the MSA, and the MSA to refine the pair representation via the outer product mean.

### 2.1 Building the features

The feature builders that turn raw inputs (an integer-encoded sequence, a stack of aligned MSA sequences, and a set of template structures) into embedding-ready tensors live in <CodeRef file="minalphafold/data.py" symbol="build_target_feat" />. Most of this is one-hot encoding and concatenation, but these tensors define what information the network receives. The main features are:

- <CodeRef file="minalphafold/data.py" symbol="build_target_feat" /> takes the target integer-encoded sequence and produces a per-residue feature vector: a one-hot over the 21 amino-acid types (20 + unknown), plus a per-residue "deletion value" and a few binary flags. Dimension 22.
- <CodeRef file="minalphafold/data.py" symbol="build_msa_feat" /> takes the full MSA and produces a $(s, r, 49)$ tensor: for each sequence and each residue, a one-hot over 23 MSA tokens (the 20 amino acids, plus gap, mask, and unknown), a cluster profile giving the average across sequences in that cluster, plus features recording which residues had gap deletions and how many. This is where evolutionary information enters the model.
- <CodeRef file="minalphafold/data.py" symbol="build_extra_msa_feat" /> does the same thing for the "extra MSA" stack, a larger, cheaper-to-process slice of the alignment that gets its own shallower Evoformer (covered in §8).

### 2.2 The input embedder

Once we have the feature tensors, the <CodeRef file="minalphafold/embedders.py" symbol="InputEmbedder" /> block projects them into $m$ and $z$ (Algorithm 3 of the supplement). The block uses three linear projections of the target features plus an outer-sum to produce a per-pair embedding for $z$, and one linear projection of the MSA features to produce $m$. It also adds a tiled copy of the target features across all MSA rows.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/embedders.py" symbol="InputEmbedder" />

The embedder initializes the pair representation from an outer-sum of the target features, *not* from the MSA. The MSA features arrive later through the outer product mean inside the Evoformer. Right after the embedder, $z$ contains target-sequence information; the Evoformer will add information about co-evolution. The embedder also tiles the target features across *every* row of $m$, including rows corresponding to other MSA sequences. This tells each row, "this is your target," which gives the row attention and column attention operations a shared coordinate system.

### 2.3 Relative position encoding

The second piece of the input-embedding stage is the relative position encoding (Algorithm 4). Like the positional encoding in a vanilla Transformer, this tells the network where each residue sits in the chain. But AlphaFold2 encodes *relative* positions rather than absolute ones, which fits the problem better: what matters structurally is whether two residues are adjacent, nearby, or far apart along the chain, not their absolute index in some fixed-length array.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/embedders.py" symbol="RelPos" />

The encoding is a clipped one-hot over $(j - i)$ within a window of $\pm 32$ residues, with anything further away in the edge bins. We linearly project this one-hot vector to $c_z$ and add it directly into $z$. Without it, the pair representation has no built-in notion of sequence adjacency, and the model would have to rediscover the protein chain from the other pair features alone.

## 3. The Evoformer — bird's-eye view

The Evoformer is the core of AlphaFold2. It is a stack of 48 blocks, each one taking an MSA representation $m$ and a pair representation $z$, applying a fixed sequence of updates, and passing the refined $(m, z)$ to the next block. By the end of the stack, $m$ and $z$ contain the structural information that the Structure Module uses to predict atom coordinates.

The diagram below shows one block: two streams running left-to-right, seven sub-blocks in temporal order, and two cross-stream arrows showing where the streams exchange information.

<EvoformerDiagram />

<Snippet file="minalphafold/evoformer.py" symbol="Evoformer" />

Each block does, in order: (1) MSA row-wise attention *biased by* $z$, (2) MSA column-wise attention, (3) an MSA transition MLP, (4) an outer product mean that writes MSA information back into $z$, (5) triangle multiplicative updates (outgoing and incoming) on $z$, (6) triangle self-attention (starting-node and ending-node) on $z$, and (7) a pair transition MLP. Every sub-block uses a dropout pattern appropriate to its tensor dimensions.

<EditorialNote>
  Of the seven sub-blocks, only the
  first three operate on $m$; the rest operate on $z$ or write into it. The
  MSA stack is relatively narrow and mostly one-way: MSA → pair via the outer
  product mean. The pair stack uses triangle consistency, triangle-biased
  attention, and pairwise transitions to refine the residue-pair features.
</EditorialNote>

The MSA stack terminates at the end of the Evoformer. The model projects only the first row of $m$, corresponding to the target sequence, into the single representation and passes it to the Structure Module. The other $s-1$ rows have pushed their evolutionary signal through the outer product mean and into $z$. Recycling (§9) wraps around the whole model. The output of block 48 becomes part of the input of block 1 in the next cycle, and this can happen up to four times per forward pass in the paper setup. Hence, "48 blocks" is really "up to 192 blocks of compute," but the recycling cycles share weights.

## 4. MSA row attention with pair bias

This is Algorithm 7 of the supplement, and it is one of the most important operations in the model. Row-wise attention means that, for each sequence $k$ in the MSA, attention runs along the row: residues of the same sequence attend to each other. The "with pair bias" part means that a learned bias term carries information from $z$ into the attention weights.

For a sequence $k$, the attention weight between residues $i$ and $j$ is:

$$
a_{sij}^h = \mathrm{softmax}_j\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\, q^h_{si} \cdot k^h_{sj} + b^h_{ij}\right)
$$

where $q^h$ and $k^h$ are query and key projections of $m$ (sequence $s$, residue $i$, head $h$), and $b^h_{ij}$ is a per-head bias term from a linear projection of $z_{ij}$. There's no $s$ subscript on the bias: all sequences in the MSA share it, because the pair representation is about the target, not about any one alignment row.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/evoformer.py" symbol="MSARowAttentionWithPairBias" />

In the main Evoformer stack, the pair representation enters the MSA stack through this bias. That is the communication channel from $z$ back into $m$. Without the bias, the model would process each row of the MSA mostly on its own terms and have a much weaker way to use current pairwise beliefs to guide residue-level attention. With the bias, every head gets a per-pair signal that adjusts attention between positions $i$ and $j$ according to the current pair representation. As the Evoformer iterates and $z$ accumulates structural information through outer product mean updates, triangle updates, and recycling, the bias becomes more informative and row attention becomes progressively more structure-aware.

<EditorialNote>
  The gating term, a sigmoid on a learned linear projection, matters too.
  The gate lets each head decide how much of its proposed update to write
  back into the residual stream.
  The paper does not isolate gating as its own ablation, but the axial-attention
  ablation removes triangles, pair bias, and gating together and reduces
  prediction accuracy. These gates help stabilize the Evoformer.
</EditorialNote>

## 5. Column attention, transition, and the outer product mean

Row attention updates residue features using information from the same sequence. The next three sub-blocks combine information *across* sequences, update the features with a transition MLP, and pass the resulting evolutionary information into $z$.

Row attention gathers information from other positions in the same sequence. Column attention gathers information from other sequences at the same aligned position. ### 5.1 MSA column-wise attention (Algorithm 8)

Column attention is the dual of row attention. For each residue position $i$, attention runs *down* the column, and sequences at the same aligned position attend to each other. For residue $i$ in sequence $k$, column attention updates the embedding using information from residue $i$ in sequences $1, \dots, s$, including sequence $k$ itself.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/embedders.py" symbol="MSAColumnAttention" />

There is no pair bias here. The pair representation is a residue-pair object, while column attention is happening across sequences at the same residue position. This is gated multi-head attention along the $s$ axis rather than the $r$ axis. Column attention lets the model ask, "what does residue 47 look like across this whole family of homologs?" and compress the answer into the target row's representation.

### 5.2 MSA transition (Algorithm 9)

A standard two-layer MLP operating per-residue, per-sequence. The hidden dimension is 4× the input dimension (the same 4× expansion pattern as a Transformer FFN), and the nonlinearity is ReLU.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/embedders.py" symbol="MSATransition" />

The transition is the MSA stack's feed-forward computation step, analogous to the FFN in a standard Transformer block. It gives the network capacity to combine the features from row and column attention.

### 5.3 The outer product mean (Algorithm 10)

The outer product mean takes the MSA representation, which row attention, column attention, and the transition MLP have updated by this point in the block, and converts it into an update for the pair representation. Its job is to take everything the model has learned about residues across the aligned sequences and distill it into a signal about each residue pair $(i, j)$ in the target sequence. The outer product mean adds that signal into $z$, where the pair stack can use it to reason about contacts.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/embedders.py" symbol="OuterProductMean" />

We start from the MSA representation of shape $(s, r, c_m)$. After a LayerNorm, we apply two separate linear projections to produce a "left" tensor $A$ and a "right" tensor $B$, each of shape $(s, r, c')$, where $c'$ is a smaller hidden dimension. You can think of $A$ as the representation of residue $i$ "as a left-hand partner" and $B$ as the representation of residue $j$ "as a right-hand partner", two different views of the same residue, each tuned for its role in the pairwise interaction.

For every sequence $k$ in the MSA and every pair of residue positions $(i, j)$, we form the outer product of $A_{k,i}$ and $B_{k,j}$. That outer product is a $c' \times c'$ matrix capturing every pairwise interaction between the features of residue $i$ and the features of residue $j$, as seen in sequence $k$. A dot product would collapse this relationship down to a single number. The outer product preserves the full feature-by-feature interaction grid.

We average these outer products over the sequences that have valid entries at both positions, ignoring padding. This gives each residue pair a summary of the learned feature products across the alignment. Averaging alone does not isolate co-evolution: independent features can also have a nonzero average product. The projections and surrounding network learn which patterns help predict structure.

The resulting tensor has shape $(r, r, c', c')$. We flatten the last two dimensions to get $(r, r, c' \cdot c')$, then apply a final linear projection to map down to $c_z$, the channel dimension of the pair representation. We can add the resulting tensor of shape $(r, r, c_z)$ directly into $z$.

The example below follows one pair of positions across six sequences. Play the animation or choose a sequence to see its two feature vectors, their outer product, and the running mean together. Inspect a matrix cell to follow its arithmetic. These are learned feature products; the result is not a contact probability or a centered covariance.

## 6. Triangle multiplicative updates

After the MSA stack has finished (row attention, column attention, transition, and the outer product mean), the pair representation $z$ holds a first pass at the evolutionary signal. For every residue pair $(i, j)$ in the target, $z_{ij}$ is a vector encoding what the model currently believes about that pair. The rest of the Evoformer block refines these pair features to help the Structure Module predict a consistent 3D structure, beginning with the two triangle multiplicative updates.

Think of $z$ as a directed graph on residues. Residues are vertices, and the pair representation $z_{ij}$ is the vector sitting on the directed edge from $i$ to $j$. Physical contacts imply consistency constraints over these edges. If residue $i$ and residue $k$ are close, and residue $k$ and residue $j$ are close, then residue $i$ and residue $j$ are also more likely to be close, because all three residues may occupy the same local region of 3D space. If you update every edge independently, each edge may be locally plausible while the triangles do not close.

For each target edge $z_{ij}$, the triangle multiplicative update sums over every residue $k$, multiplying together the other two edges of a triangle $(i, k, j)$:

<Equation id="eq-trimul-outgoing" number="11">
$$
z_{ij} \leftarrow g_{ij} \odot \mathrm{Linear}\!\left( \sum_k a_{ik} \odot b_{jk} \right)
$$
</Equation>

That's the **outgoing** variant. Two linear projections of $z$ produce $a$ and $b$. You can think of $a_{ik}$ as "the view of the $i \to k$ edge we use to update outgoing edges at $i$" and $b_{jk}$ as "the view of the $j \to k$ edge for the same purpose." For each $k$, the elementwise product $a_{ik} \odot b_{jk}$ combines these two views. We then sum over every $k$, linearly project back to pair-rep dimension, gate with a learned sigmoid $g_{ij}$, and add into $z$.

The **incoming** variant is the symmetric move, operating on columns instead of rows:

<Equation id="eq-trimul-incoming" number="12">
$$
z_{ij} \leftarrow g_{ij} \odot \mathrm{Linear}\!\left( \sum_k a_{ki} \odot b_{kj} \right)
$$
</Equation>

Same operation, different triangle. In outgoing, we're using the edges emanating *from* $i$ and *from* $j$ to $k$; in incoming, we're using the edges arriving *at* $i$ and *at* $j$ from $k$. Together, they cover both directional structures the pair representation is asymmetric in.

## 7. Triangle self-attention

The triangle *multiplicative* updates enforce triangle consistency in a direct algebraic way: they refine every edge using a sum over third vertices. The triangle *self-attention* layers are the softer, attention-weighted version of the same idea. They let each edge attend to other edges that share a residue with it, using learned compatibility scores that also reflect the current pair representation.

The two variants differ in which residue the edges share.

**Starting-node attention (Algorithm 13).** For each target edge $(i, j)$, the query comes from $z_{ij}$, and the keys and values come from the set of edges starting at $i$, namely $\{z_{ik} : k \in [1, r]\}$. The attention scores also get a bias term derived from $z_{jk}$, so the third edge of the triangle enters the attention pattern directly.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/embedders.py" symbol="TriangleAttentionStartingNode" />

**Ending-node attention (Algorithm 14).** The same thing, but now the keys and values come from the edges ending at $j$, namely $\{z_{kj} : k \in [1, r]\}$, and the bias comes from $z_{ki}$.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/embedders.py" symbol="TriangleAttentionEndingNode" />

The two operations together cover both ways a third residue can share a node with the edge $(i, j)$: either $k$ sits at the start of both $(i, k)$ and $(i, j)$, or $k$ sits at the end of both $(k, j)$ and $(i, j)$. The triangle bias pulls the third edge of the triangle directly into the softmax. Without that bias, the operations would look like ordinary attention over rows and columns of $z$.

## 8. Extra MSA and templates

The extra-MSA and template processing steps add information to the pair representation before the main Evoformer runs. Both use smaller stacks to produce these updates; the 48-block Evoformer then refines the MSA and pair representations.

### 8.1 Extra MSA

AlphaFold2 clusters its main MSA input and caps the number of sequences to carry rich signal while keeping the Evoformer's attention cost manageable. The **extra MSA** is a larger slice of the same alignment. A smaller version of the Evoformer processes it with fewer blocks and global column attention instead of full column attention, then adds the result to the pair representation.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/embedders.py" symbol="ExtraMsaStack" />

Where the main MSA uses full column attention, the extra MSA uses <CodeRef file="minalphafold/embedders.py" symbol="MSAColumnGlobalAttention" />, which computes attention from a pooled summary of each column rather than from all pairwise sequence-to-sequence scores. As in many efficient-attention methods, this trades some expressivity for a much cheaper operation. The extra MSA can afford that tradeoff because it only needs to extract more evolutionary signal and write it into $z$, without keeping a detailed representation of each sequence.

### 8.2 Templates

When AlphaFold2 has structural hints from related proteins in the PDB, it processes them with a dedicated template pair stack. It featurizes each template into a pair representation of shape $(r, r, c_t)$: distances between backbone atoms, unit vectors between Cα positions, sequence identity masks, and related geometric features. The template pair stack then passes these features through a few Evoformer-style blocks specialized for pair-only updates.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/embedders.py" symbol="TemplatePair" />

The template pair stack uses the pair operations from the Evoformer: triangle multiplication (outgoing and incoming), triangle attention (starting and ending), and a pair transition, with no MSA side. The stack outputs a pair tensor for each template. A <CodeRef file="minalphafold/embedders.py" symbol="TemplatePointwiseAttention" /> layer combines those tensors across templates, letting the target sequence attend across its available structural hints.

## 9. Recycling

The last structural idea in the Evoformer-level description of AlphaFold2 is recycling. Run a forward pass to get an MSA representation, a pair representation, and a Structure Module output. Then take parts of those outputs, feed them back into the *inputs* of the next forward pass, and run the whole model again. In the paper setup, this happens up to four times at inference and a sampled number of times during training.

<RecyclingDiagram />

The model feeds back three things: the first row of the final MSA representation ($m^{(1)}$, the target row), the final pair representation $z$, and a distance-binned version of the pseudo-Cβ coordinates from the predicted structure. It normalizes the representation tensors and adds them into the current-cycle input embeddings. The pseudo-Cβ distances go through one extra step: `recycling_distance_bin` one-hot-encodes pairwise distances into 15 radial bins, linearly projects the bins into pair-representation dimension, and adds the result into $z$.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/model.py" lines="262-272" />

**Stop-gradient between cycles.** The model detaches the feedback tensors from the computation graph. Gradients only flow through the final cycle of a training forward pass; earlier cycles provide refinement but do not receive direct gradient through the recycling loop. This keeps the memory cost much lower than fully unrolling every cycle. It also has a useful regularizing effect: the model has to learn representations that both the current cycle's Structure Module and the next cycle's input embedder can use.

**Random number of cycles during training.** At inference, we can fix the number of cycles. During training, we sample it uniformly, so the model sees 1-cycle, 2-cycle, and later-cycle inputs and must produce reasonable predictions in all of them rather than specializing only to the final cycle. This training regime teaches the model to converge iteratively.

<EditorialNote>
  I find recycling to be one of the most underrated components of AlphaFold2.
  It is the mechanism that turns a 48-block Evoformer into an iterative
  refiner with shared weights. The inference cost rises with the number of
  cycles, but the paper's ablations show that removing recycling is one of the
  larger reductions in accuracy. The model learns to use its own
  previous predictions, so later cycles operate
  on inputs that are increasingly close to the distribution the model itself
  creates.
</EditorialNote>

## 10. The Structure Module

Input embedding, the Evoformer, and recycling produce two useful representations: a pair representation $z$ rich with structural information, and a single representation $s_i$ for each residue of the target sequence. The Structure Module uses these to place atoms in 3D space.

Unlike the Evoformer's generic self-attention over abstract sequence tensors, the Structure Module operates on **rigid frames**, local coordinate systems attached to each residue. Its attention weights and coordinate updates preserve the underlying geometry when the protein's global orientation changes. That SE(3) constraint determines the form of the Structure Module.

The module runs for 8 shared-weight iterations. Each iteration performs invariant point attention, a transition MLP, a backbone update, and all-atom coordinate construction. Before the full loop in §10e, we'll work through the geometry.

### 10a. Rigid frames

Every residue in an AlphaFold2 prediction has an associated **rigid frame**: a translation $t_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and a rotation $R_i \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$. Together, $T_i = (R_i, t_i)$ is an element of the special Euclidean group $\mathrm{SE}(3)$, the group of rigid motions in 3D. You can think of $T_i$ as a small coordinate system attached to the residue. The origin sits on the residue's $C_\alpha$ atom; the $x$ axis points roughly along the $C_\alpha \to C$ bond; the $y$ axis lives in the plane of $N$, $C_\alpha$, and $C$ while remaining orthogonal to $x$; and the $z$ axis is their cross product.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/geometry.py" lines="219-251" title="rigid_frame_from_three_points" />

The function computes frames from triples of atom coordinates. In AlphaFold2, these are the $N$, $C_\alpha$, and $C$ backbone atoms. The construction is a three-step Gram-Schmidt procedure: translate so $C_\alpha$ is at the origin, orient $x$ along $C_\alpha \to C$, and orient $y$ by projecting $N$ into the $xy$-plane. The specific convention matters because it must match the convention used later to build side chains.

Why carry around frames rather than just Cartesian coordinates? First, frames express geometry *relatively*. For any two residues $i$ and $j$, the position of $j$ "as seen by $i$" is just $T_i^{-1} \cdot t_j$, meaning residue $j$'s translation expressed in residue $i$'s local coordinate system. This operation is invariant under any global rigid motion of the whole protein, and that invariance is something we want to build in rather than learn. Second, frames compose cleanly. If you have an update $\Delta T$ to apply to a residue, the composition $T_i \leftarrow T_i \cdot \Delta T$ is still a valid frame. This is how backbone updates work in §10c, and how the model builds side-chain atoms by composing torsion-angle rotations with literature frames in §10d.

#### Quaternions as the rotation parameterization

Rotations have three natural representations: rotation matrices (9 numbers, 6 constraints from orthogonality), Euler angles (3 numbers, but with problems such as gimbal lock and discontinuous wrap-around), and quaternions (4 numbers, 1 constraint from unit norm). AlphaFold2 uses quaternions when it *outputs* or *updates* a rotation, and rotation matrices when it *applies* one.

Quaternions allow smooth updates. When the Structure Module emits a backbone update, it outputs three scalars, which we can call $(b, c, d)$, and forms an unnormalized quaternion $q = (1, b, c, d)$. It then normalizes this quaternion and converts it to a rotation matrix. This parameterization has a useful property: the identity rotation is at $q = (1, 0, 0, 0)$, corresponding to $(b, c, d) = (0, 0, 0)$. Thus, "no update" is the all-zero model output, which is exactly what we want a small MLP to default to at initialization. Rotation matrices and Euler angles do not give us this nearly as cleanly.

A unit quaternion $q = (w, x, y, z)$ maps to a rotation matrix via:

<Equation id="eq-quat-mat" number="quat→R">
$$
R(q) = \begin{pmatrix}
1 - 2(y^2 + z^2) & 2(xy - wz) & 2(xz + wy) \\
2(xy + wz) & 1 - 2(x^2 + z^2) & 2(yz - wx) \\
2(xz - wy) & 2(yz + wx) & 1 - 2(x^2 + y^2)
\end{pmatrix}
$$
</Equation>

Scaling a nonzero quaternion leaves the rotation unchanged after normalization, and $q$ and $-q$ describe the same rotation. A 90° turn about X uses $q=(1/\sqrt{2},1/\sqrt{2},0,0)$ because the quaternion uses half the rotation angle.

### 10b. Invariant Point Attention

Invariant Point Attention is the central operation in the Structure Module. It uses rigid frames to define a three-term attention score that is invariant to any global rigid motion of the input. Rotating or translating the whole protein leaves the IPA attention scores unchanged.

The score between a query residue $i$ and a key residue $j$ for head $h$ looks like:

<Equation id="eq-ipa-score" number="22">
$$
a^h_{ij} \propto \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\, q^h_i \cdot k^h_j}_{\text{scalar}} + \underbrace{b^h_{ij}}_{\text{pair bias}} - \underbrace{\frac{\gamma^h}{\sqrt{2\,c_p}} \sum_{p=1}^{c_p} \left\| T_i \cdot \vec{q}^{\,h,p}_i - T_j \cdot \vec{k}^{\,h,p}_j \right\|^2}_{\text{point distance}}
$$
</Equation>

The **scalar** term is a standard attention score on scalar queries and keys, analogous to attention in the Evoformer. The **pair bias** term injects the current pair representation $z_{ij}$ into the attention pattern, just like row attention did in §4. The **point distance** term accounts for the geometry.

Each query head carries a small bank of 3D **query points** $\vec{q}^{\,h,p}_i$ in residue $i$'s *local* frame; applying $T_i$ lifts them into global coordinates. The same is true for the key points $\vec{k}^{\,h,p}_j$. The point-distance term sums the negative squared distances between pairs of globally transformed points. If a query point lifted from $i$ ends up close in 3D to a key point lifted from $j$, that residue pair gets a higher attention score.

Why does this give invariance? Suppose we apply a global rigid motion $G \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$ to every residue. Then $T_i \mapsto G \cdot T_i$ and $T_j \mapsto G \cdot T_j$. The transformed points become $G \cdot (T_i \cdot \vec{q}^{\,h,p}_i)$ and $G \cdot (T_j \cdot \vec{k}^{\,h,p}_j)$. Rigid motions preserve distances, so the squared-distance term stays unchanged. The scalar term does not touch positions, and the pair bias comes from $z$, which has no global spatial orientation. A rigid motion therefore preserves all three terms and the attention pattern.

#### The code

<Snippet file="minalphafold/structure_module.py" symbol="InvariantPointAttention" />

The linear projections fall into three groups: scalar queries/keys/values (`linear_q`, `linear_kv`, etc.), 3D query/key/value *points* (`linear_q_points`, `linear_kv_points`), and the gate. The scalar projections produce tensors of shape $(r, h, c)$; the point projections produce $(r, h, 3, c_p)$, where the extra dimension is 3D space. `_project_points` takes the point tensors and applies each residue's frame to them: the $T_i \cdot q$ operation in the equation. The score sums the Q·K dot product, pair bias projection, and squared-distance sum across points, with a learnable per-head weight $\gamma^h$ controlling the point contribution.

The output is more elaborate than in standard attention. IPA returns (1) a weighted sum of scalar values, which is the regular attention output; (2) a weighted sum of value points, giving a point-cloud-like output with one 3D vector per head; and (3) scalar features derived from the attention-weighted norms of those value points. It concatenates these and projects them back into the single representation $s_i$.

<EditorialNote>
  IPA combines scalar attention, pair bias, and distances between 3D points in
  local residue frames. This construction was one of AlphaFold2's main
  architectural contributions and influenced later geometry-aware attention
  mechanisms in structure-prediction systems.
</EditorialNote>

### 10c. Backbone update

IPA and its transition MLP produce an updated single representation $s_i$ for each residue. The backbone update turns this $s_i$ into an update on the frame $T_i$: project $s_i$ to six scalars, interpret the first three as the imaginary part of an unnormalized quaternion, interpret the last three as a translation, and compose the resulting rigid transformation into $T_i$.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/structure_module.py" symbol="BackboneUpdate" />

Rather than projecting $s_i$ to four scalars $(w, x, y, z)$, AlphaFold2 projects to three scalars $(b, c, d)$ and forms $q = (1, b, c, d)$. At initialization, the projection is near zero, so $(b, c, d) \approx (0, 0, 0)$ and $q \approx (1, 0, 0, 0)$, the identity quaternion. The update therefore begins near the identity rotation and learns corrections from that stable starting point.

The backbone update is additive in the space of *rotations*, not in the space of quaternion components. The composition $T_i \leftarrow T_i \cdot (R(q), t_{\text{pred}})$ multiplies rotations (because $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ isn't a vector space) and adds translations. In the QuaternionFrame widget from §10a, set $(b, c, d)$ to small values and watch the rotation matrix stay close to identity.

### 10d. All-atom coordinates

A per-residue backbone frame gives us only part of the predicted protein structure. We need all the atoms: backbone atoms (N, Cα, C, O) plus whatever side-chain atoms the residue type has. For the 20 standard amino acids, the codebase uses at most 14 atoms per residue, the "atom14" representation.

AlphaFold2 reduces the remaining degrees of freedom to **seven torsion angles** per residue: the three backbone dihedrals ($\phi$, $\psi$, $\omega$) plus up to four $\chi$ side-chain angles. The residue type and these torsion angles determine the full all-atom structure up to fixed chemical geometry. Bond lengths and bond angles come from literature values in `residue_constants.py`; the model has to predict the torsions.

AlphaFold2 predicts the seven torsions per residue with a small head attached to the Structure Module output, then composes a chain of small rigid-body transformations to produce the atom coordinates:

<Snippet file="minalphafold/structure_module.py" lines="782-839" title="compute_all_atom_coordinates" />

For each residue, the model assembles eight **rigid groups**:

1. Backbone (N, Cα, C, O): sits in the residue's backbone frame $T_i$ directly.
2. Group for $\chi_1$: a rotation around a literature-defined axis by the predicted $\chi_1$ angle positions this group and its literature-defined atoms.
3. Group for $\chi_2$: another torsion rotation positions this group relative to group $\chi_1$.
4. Groups for $\chi_3$, $\chi_4$: same pattern.
5. Three more groups for secondary geometry (e.g., ring planes).

<Snippet file="minalphafold/structure_module.py" lines="714-779" title="rigid_group_frames_from_torsions" />

The `make_rot_x` helper rotates around the local $x$-axis by a learned angle. Encoding this as sine and cosine, rather than as a raw angle, avoids wrap-around at $\pm \pi$. To compute each rigid-group frame $T^\text{group}_g$ as $T^\text{parent}_g \cdot T^\text{literature}_g \cdot \mathrm{Rot}_x(\alpha_g)$, compose the parent group's frame with the literature default transformation and then with the torsion-angle rotation.

With the eight group frames in hand, look up the atom positions in `residue_constants.py`'s `restype_atom14_rigid_group_positions` table. This table says, for each amino acid, "atom at index $k$ of atom14 sits at local coordinates $\vec{p}^{\text{aa}}_k$ in rigid group $g^{\text{aa}}_k$." Transform $\vec{p}^{\text{aa}}_k$ by the group frame and you have the atom's global position.

<EditorialNote>
  The torsion head and atom-coordinate composition are surprisingly simple
  given what they accomplish. The whole thing (8 rigid groups, 7 torsion
  angles per residue, and a lookup table of atom positions) fits in a few
  hundred lines. Chemistry supplies the bond lengths and bond angles, so the
  Structure Module only has to learn where to place each residue's backbone
  frame and which torsion angles to emit.
</EditorialNote>

### 10e. The full structure module loop

With IPA, the backbone update, and the all-atom construction in place, the Structure Module itself is a short loop: eight iterations, weights shared across iterations, with each iteration refining the backbone frames and side-chain torsions.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/structure_module.py" symbol="StructureModule" />

Every iteration applies IPA, transition, and backbone update, and all eight iterations see the same pair representation $z$. The model computes this representation once after the Evoformer and holds it fixed for the whole structure loop. The set of *frames* changes with each iteration, feeding back into IPA's point-distance term and shifting the attention pattern as the predicted structure changes. Every iteration also produces a full prediction: backbone frames and torsion-derived coordinates. During training, per-iteration auxiliary FAPE losses push the model to make every iteration structurally meaningful, not just the last one.

A vanilla encoder typically receives supervision only at its final layer. Applying FAPE at every iteration prevents early iterations from producing arbitrary intermediate frames useful only to the final iteration and trains the model to improve its prediction at each step, so it can produce a meaningful structure at every iteration during inference. With shared weights, these iterations form an unrolled refinement process.

After the loop finishes, the Structure Module emits the final frames $T_i$, the final atom14 coordinates, and the per-iteration predictions for auxiliary losses. These, plus the final single and pair representations, feed into the auxiliary heads (§11). The final pseudo-Cβ positions also feed back into the recycling loop (§9) for the next cycle.

Every head is architecturally simple: a LayerNorm, a linear projection (sometimes two), and a softmax or sigmoid. Meeting both requirements is part of why the pair stack is so expensive in the Evoformer.

## 12. Losses

AlphaFold2's output lives in 3D space, where a naive $\ell_2$ distance between predicted and true coordinates depends on the arbitrary global orientation of both structures. If you predict a structure that is correct but globally rotated 90°, coordinate MSE will assign a large error, even though the prediction is chemically and biologically the same.

AlphaFold2's central loss is **Frame-Aligned Point Error**, or FAPE. After FAPE, we will cover the remaining terms more quickly: torsion loss, pLDDT loss, distogram loss, structural violations, and the combined `AlphaFoldLoss` that weights them together.

### 12.1 FAPE

Rather than comparing predicted and true atom positions in one global coordinate system, FAPE compares them in *every residue's local frame* (§10), one at a time, and averages. For a predicted frame $T_i$ and predicted atom position $x_j$, along with their ground-truth counterparts $T_i^*$ and $x_j^*$, the per-pair FAPE error is:

<Equation id="eq-fape" number="28">
$$
d_{ij} = \left\| T_i^{-1} \cdot x_j \;-\; (T_i^*)^{-1} \cdot x_j^* \right\|
$$
</Equation>

Read the two terms from inside out. $T_i^{-1} \cdot x_j$ is "the position of atom $x_j$ as seen by residue $i$'s local frame," exactly the same local-frame operation we met in IPA (§10b). $(T_i^*)^{-1} \cdot x_j^*$ is the same thing in the ground truth structure: where residue $i$ *should* have seen atom $j$. The error $d_{ij}$ is the Euclidean distance between those two local-frame vectors.

This has two crucial properties. First, it is **SE(3) invariant by construction.** Apply any global rigid motion $G$ to the predicted structure. Frames transform as $T_i \mapsto G \cdot T_i$ and atoms as $x_j \mapsto G \cdot x_j$. The local-frame position becomes $(G \cdot T_i)^{-1} \cdot (G \cdot x_j) = T_i^{-1} \cdot G^{-1} \cdot G \cdot x_j = T_i^{-1} \cdot x_j$. The loss does not penalize global rotations or translations. Second, it is **per-residue**: the error compares residue $i$'s predicted local view to residue $i$'s true local view.

Use the sliders below to rotate the prediction or move a single residue. The presets provide four starting points for comparison. Gray is the reference; green is the prediction. Rotating the prediction changes coordinate MSE, while FAPE stays fixed to numerical precision. Moving one residue changes the local geometry, so FAPE detects an error. This example uses a synthetic 3D chain, with one point per residue, and clamps FAPE distances at 3 arbitrary length units, without AlphaFold2’s length-scale normalization.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/losses.py" lines="49-126" title="frame_aligned_point_error" />

Two implementation details matter here.

**The distance clamp.** The raw error can be arbitrarily large. If the model gets a structure very wrong, some local-frame distances can grow to hundreds of angstroms. FAPE clamps at `d_clamp`, default 10 Å. Without clamping, the gradient from a small number of badly misplaced atoms can dominate the whole update. Clamping first pressures the model to bring all atoms within the relevant neighborhood before rewarding it for making already-close atoms even closer.

**The normalization.** FAPE divides the sum of clamped distances by `d_clamp × mask.sum()`, so the loss is dimensionless and in [0, 1]. Keeping every term on the same scale matters when choosing weights for the combined loss.

### 12.2 Per-iteration backbone FAPE

The Structure Module's 8 iterations each produce a set of backbone frames (§10e). Training evaluates FAPE on *every* iteration's output and averages the per-iteration losses into a single "backbone trajectory" loss.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/losses.py" symbol="BackboneTrajectoryLoss" />

This pressures every iteration to produce a structurally reasonable prediction. Without it, early iterations could emit arbitrary intermediate frames that are useful only to later iterations. Per-iteration FAPE trains each iteration to refine the structure incrementally.

### 12.3 All-atom FAPE

Once the Structure Module has emitted its last set of backbone frames and the all-atom reconstruction (§10d) has placed every side-chain atom, all-atom FAPE evaluates the full atom14 tensor: every backbone and side-chain atom of every residue, as seen by every residue's backbone frame.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/losses.py" symbol="AllAtomFAPE" />

The reference frames for this loss come from the *true* backbone atoms, not the predicted ones. And since some residues have symmetric side chains, Algorithm 26 of the supplement "renames" the ground truth to match whichever chemically equivalent atom permutation the model predicted. That permutation search runs before this loss, so by the time FAPE runs, the predicted and true atom tensors are in the right correspondence.

### 12.4 Torsion angle loss

Side-chain torsions ($\chi_1$ through $\chi_4$) and backbone torsions ($\phi$, $\psi$, $\omega$) need their own supervision. Torsion angles live on the circle $S^1$, so the naive approach, L2 on the raw angle, has a discontinuity at $\pm \pi$. AlphaFold2 avoids this by predicting $(\sin\alpha, \cos\alpha)$ pairs and supervising those:

<Snippet file="minalphafold/losses.py" symbol="TorsionAngleLoss" />

The loss has two terms: $\ell_2$ between the predicted $(\sin, \cos)$ pair and the ground truth pair, plus a penalty that encourages the predicted pair to lie on the unit circle, meaning $\sqrt{\sin^2 + \cos^2} = 1$. The unit-circle penalty matters because the model normalizes its raw 2D output vector before interpreting it as an angle. Keeping the raw output close to unit norm stabilizes the learning dynamics.

### 12.5 pLDDT, distogram, experimentally-resolved, violations

Four more losses round out the full objective. Most are classification losses attached to the auxiliary heads from §11, plus one physical-plausibility loss.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/losses.py" symbol="PLDDTLoss" />

**pLDDT** is cross-entropy on 50 lDDT-$C_\alpha$ bins. To compute lDDT, scan through $C_\alpha$ pairs within a distance cutoff and check how close the predicted distance is to the true distance. This gives a local, alignment-free fidelity measure. The pLDDT head learns to predict which lDDT bin each residue will fall into, producing the confidence scores users see on predicted structures.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/losses.py" symbol="DistogramLoss" />

**Distogram** is cross-entropy on 64 bins of $C_\beta$-$C_\beta$ distance (or pseudo-$C_\beta$ for glycines). It reads from $z$, not from the predicted structure, which means the pair representation has to encode pairwise distance information *before* the Structure Module runs. This makes the distogram head a useful auxiliary signal for the pair stack.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/losses.py" symbol="ExperimentallyResolvedLoss" />

**Experimentally-resolved** is binary cross-entropy per atom. For each of the 14 possible atoms per residue, the model predicts whether the experiment resolved that atom. Loops, terminal regions, and some side chains are often missing from crystal structures, and the model needs to learn that "not resolved" is not the same as "does not exist."

<Snippet file="minalphafold/losses.py" symbol="StructuralViolationLoss" />

**Structural violations** penalizes physically implausible geometry through three components: bond length deviations, bond angle deviations, and van der Waals clashes. Each component applies a soft penalty. The paper training recipe uses this loss during fine-tuning rather than from the start, because early in training the structures are too wrong for "avoid clashes" to be a productive constraint.

### 12.6 The combined loss

`AlphaFoldLoss` combines these terms in a weighted sum:

<Snippet file="minalphafold/losses.py" symbol="AlphaFoldLoss" />

The supplement gives specific weights. During the main training stage, the loss is:

$$
0.5L_\text{FAPE} + 0.5L_\text{aux} + 0.3L_\text{dist} + 2.0L_\text{msa} + 0.01L_\text{conf}.
$$

Fine-tuning adds the experimentally-resolved and structural-violation losses with weights $0.01$ and $1.0$, respectively. Training differentiates the combined scalar loss end-to-end.

<EditorialNote>
  FAPE is the clearest example in the whole AF2 paper of making an
  architectural choice through a loss rather than a layer. You could imagine
  building a model that outputs atom coordinates and then applies some
  alignment step (like Kabsch superposition) before comparing to
  ground truth in a single global frame, as some earlier structure
  prediction work did. FAPE skips the alignment step entirely
  by constructing a loss that is <em>intrinsically</em> alignment-free.
  This loss supervises every one of the network's intermediate frames,
  and because it is SE(3)-invariant, the frames can
  live in <em>any</em> global orientation the model finds convenient during
  training.
</EditorialNote>

## 13. Training

minAlphaFold2 covers cropping, recycling sampling, feature construction, collation, and gradient checkpointing, but omits one major component: self-distillation.

### 13.1 Feature engineering

The training-time additions are the supervision builders, which turn raw `.mmcif` files into all the targets required by the losses in §12:

After that call, the data processing code extracts atom14 coordinates and masks from the experimental structure, builds ground-truth backbone frames via `rigid_frame_from_three_points`, computes torsion angles from $\phi$/$\psi$/$\omega$/$\chi_{1..4}$, and produces the supervised tensors that the losses consume. The output is a single processed-example dictionary for the remaining processing steps.

### 13.2 Cropping

Real proteins can be thousands of residues long. The full Evoformer at paper-spec config, with 48 blocks and triangle multiplication's $O(r^3)$ scaling, becomes extremely expensive as $r$ grows. AlphaFold2 **trains on crops** by sampling random contiguous windows of a fixed length $N_\text{crop}$, usually 256 residues, from each protein.

<Snippet file="minalphafold/data.py" lines="317-362" title="crop_example" />

If the chain is shorter than the crop size, pass it through unchanged. Otherwise, pick one contiguous residue window and slice every residue-indexed field consistently: target sequence, MSA columns, template coordinates, atom14 supervision, and masks. During training, `_crop_start` samples the start index uniformly; at inference, it centers the window deterministically.

The model often trains on pieces of proteins but must generalize to full-length proteins at inference. Empirically, the triangle-consistency inductive biases in the Evoformer and the geometry of the Structure Module are strong enough that local structural relationships learned on crops compose reasonably well.

### 13.3 Batch collation

Some proteins are shorter than the crop size, so we need padding and masking to batch variable-length crops. `collate_batch` does both:

<Snippet file="minalphafold/data.py" lines="1242-1425" title="collate_batch" />

`collate_batch` pads every feature tensor to the largest example in the batch. A `seq_mask` tensor blocks out padded positions in the attention operations: attention scores at padded indices go to $-\infty$ before softmax, and the pipeline ignores outputs at padded positions before computing the loss.

### 13.4 Recycling sampling

Recycling feeds previous-cycle outputs back into current-cycle inputs and detaches gradients between cycles (§9). During training, we **sample the number of cycles uniformly**:

```python
n_cycles = random.randint(1, max_cycles)  # uniform during training
```

This forces the model to produce usable representations at many cycle counts, not just the maximum. A model trained only with 4 cycles could specialize to the 4-cycle case. With uniform cycle sampling, the weights must handle 1-cycle, 2-cycle, ..., $n$-cycle inputs reasonably well.

### 13.5 Gradient checkpointing

The full Evoformer at 48 blocks, with recycling and triangle multiplication's $O(r^3)$ cost, has an activation memory footprint that can easily dominate the parameter memory. PyTorch's `torch.utils.checkpoint` trades compute for memory by not storing intermediate activations during the forward pass. During the backward pass, it re-runs the forward for each checkpointed block to reconstruct those activations.

In minAlphaFold2, gradient checkpointing wraps every Evoformer block and every extra-MSA block. For a model that repeats its expensive parts dozens of times, this memory saving makes the difference between a runnable training script and one that runs out of memory immediately.

<span id="136-the-honest-gap-self-distillation" aria-hidden="true" />

### 13.6 Self-distillation

The full AlphaFold2 training procedure has one piece that minAlphaFold2 does not implement: **self-distillation**. In the full procedure, an undistilled model trains on known PDB structures, then predicts structures for large numbers of unlabeled protein sequences from Uniclust30. The training procedure retains high-confidence predicted structures as pseudo-ground-truth and mixes them with real PDB structures.

In the supplement, a KL-based confidence metric computed from predicted aligned error-like distributions filters the distillation set; the authors note that pLDDT would likely work as well or better. Self-distillation lets the model learn from unlabeled sequence space, but it requires substantial infrastructure: two-stage training, a massive prediction run, and careful filtering of pseudo-labels.

<EditorialNote>
  minAlphaFold2 deliberately omits self-distillation. It matters for final
  accuracy, but it is mostly a data-generation and training-infrastructure
  problem rather than a new architectural idea. Including it faithfully
  would add a large amount of pipeline code without changing the conceptual account of Evoformer,
  recycling, IPA, or FAPE. This implementation therefore does not fully
  reproduce DeepMind's training recipe.
</EditorialNote>

## 14. A real-world walkthrough

The cleanest test of whether the pipeline works end-to-end is a **single-protein overfit**. Pick one PDB, feed it to the model as both input sequence and target coordinates, and train until the model memorizes the structure. If the features, Evoformer, Structure Module, loss, and optimizer work together correctly, the predicted coordinates should move toward the ground truth even on CPU. If something important is wrong, such as a sign error in a frame update or a loss using the wrong coordinate system, the overfit will usually fail.

### 14.1 The setup

The protein I'm using is **1CRN**, crambin, a 46-residue plant protein with three disulfide bonds. It is small enough to train on a laptop CPU in under a minute, but geometrically nontrivial enough that a random-initialized model gets it very wrong at step 0 and recognizably right by step 600.

The driver script lives at `scripts/overfit_single_pdb.py` in the minAlphaFold repo. Simplified to the essentials:

```python
parsed = parse_pdb("1crn.pdb")
example = build_minimal_example("1crn", parsed)
batch = collate_batch([example], crop_size=n_res, msa_depth=1,
                      extra_msa_depth=0, max_templates=0, ...)

model = AlphaFold2(load_model_config("tiny"))    # 0.09M params, 1 Evoformer block
loss_fn = AlphaFoldLoss(finetune=False)
optimizer = build_optimizer(model, TrainingConfig(learning_rate=1e-3, ...))

for step in range(1, 601):
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    outputs = model(**model_inputs_from_batch(batch, tc))
    loss = loss_fn(**loss_inputs_from_batch(batch, outputs)).mean()
    loss.backward()
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 0.1)
    optimizer.step()
```

The MSA has exactly one sequence, the query itself, and we disable templates and extra MSA. We are running the **tiny** profile, which has about 90k parameters, 1 Evoformer block, and a shallow Structure Module, not full paper-spec AlphaFold2. And we train with the same sequence and target at every step, so the model memorizes one structure.

<EditorialNote>
  A 90k-parameter model converging toward the right geometry after 600 steps
  on one protein tests whether gradient descent on FAPE can drive
  coordinates toward ground truth. This is a low bar, but a necessary one
  that catches an enormous number of bugs. A falling loss alone is too weak
  a test; I want to see the predicted structure start looking like the real one.
</EditorialNote>

Across the checkpoints:

- **Step 0-10**: the prediction is essentially random, with Cα RMSD about 9.7 Å. The Structure Module has produced 46 backbone frames from random weights, so there is no reason yet to expect a protein-like structure.
- **Step 10-100**: the model starts to contract and organize. FAPE's gradient is pulling each residue toward consistency with many local-frame views of the ground truth. The prediction is still quite wrong, but it is less diffuse.

### 14.3 What this does and doesn't tell us

The overfit shows that **the components work together correctly**: the feature builders produce the right inputs, the Evoformer maps them through, the Structure Module produces frames, FAPE computes a useful gradient, the backbone update and all-atom reconstruction compose into plausible coordinates, and optimization drives all of this toward the ground truth. A single forward-backward loop exercises every major code path from §§2-13, and the final structure looks like a real protein.

The model has seen exactly one protein, so step 600's 2.25 Å Cα RMSD measures memorization and gives no evidence of **generalization**. Real AlphaFold2 training uses known PDB structures, deep MSAs, templates, self-distillation on large unlabeled sequence sets, and enormous compute.

The overfit is still the most satisfying "does my code actually work?" demo I know of: seeing a structure move from random geometry into crambin shows why the loss is falling.

To run it yourself:


## 15. What AlphaFold2 left unsolved

AlphaFold2 substantially improved monomeric protein structure prediction for proteins with reasonable MSA coverage. Several problems remained:

**Protein complexes.** Proteins form complexes with other proteins, DNA, RNA, small molecules, ions, and membranes. AF2 primarily addressed monomeric protein structure prediction. **AlphaFold-Multimer** extended the system to complexes, and **AlphaFold3** went further with a redesigned architecture, diffusion-based output, and support for nucleic acids, ligands, and post-translational modifications.

**Dynamics.** Proteins flex and transition between conformations that may be functionally distinct. AF2 predicts a single likely structure, which is often enough for structural biology but not always enough for enzyme mechanisms, allosteric regulation, or motion-dependent function. MSA-clustering methods can produce multiple states from AF2 by perturbing the input MSA, while diffusion-based successors such as **AlphaFold3** and **Boltz-1** are better suited to sampling.

**Design.** Structure prediction is the forward problem: sequence → structure. The inverse problem, "give me a sequence that folds into this structure," is protein design. **RoseTTAFold Diffusion**, **ProteinMPNN**, and **AlphaProteo** address this or related problems. They often use AF2-descended ideas to evaluate designs, but the generative problem is different.

**Protein language models.** Some later models replace the MSA with representations from a pretrained protein language model. **ESMFold** follows this approach, relying on a protein transformer to encode sequence patterns that the MSA pipeline would otherwise supply. **ESM3** trains jointly on sequence, structure, and function. A [multi-part primer on protein language models](https://chrishayduk.com) discusses this line of work in more detail.

**Compute and inference cost.** AlphaFold2 is expensive to train and its inference pipeline includes the additional cost of MSA search. ESMFold, distillation-based approaches, and newer compute-optimized architectures address this constraint in different ways.

<EditorialNote>
  Later systems retained triangle updates, geometric reasoning, FAPE-like
  losses, and recycling, while often changing the MSA pipeline, Structure
  Module, or training procedure.
</EditorialNote>
